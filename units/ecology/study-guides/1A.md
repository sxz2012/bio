## Part 1. From the textbook

Excerpts are copied word for word from the textbooks, with page numbers so you can find them in your own copy. **Bold** marks the key sentences.

### Miller & Levine, Lesson 1.1 "What Is Science?"

**What science is** (p. 8)

> The term *science* is usually defined as the use of evidence to construct testable explanations and predictions of natural phenomena, as well as the knowledge generated through this process. **Most importantly, science is a process—an organized way of observing and asking questions about the natural world, developing those questions into testable explanations, and gathering and analyzing data that support or reject those explanations.**

**How science differs from other ways of explaining the world** (p. 9)

> First, science deals only with the natural world. … Second, scientists collect and organize information in an orderly way, looking at events for patterns and connections of cause and effect. Third, scientists propose explanations based on evidence and understanding, not belief.

**Science rarely "proves" anything** (p. 10)

> You may be surprised to hear this, but science rarely "proves" anything. Scientists aim for the best understanding of the natural world that current methods can reveal. Uncertainty is always part of the scientific process, and is part of what makes science exciting!

**Scientific methodology** (p. 10)

> There isn't a single, cut-and-dried "scientific method." But there is a general style of investigation we call scientific methodology … **Scientific methodology involves observing and asking questions, forming hypotheses, conducting controlled experiments, collecting and analyzing data, and drawing conclusions.**

**Observation, inference, hypothesis** (p. 12)

> **Observing and Asking Questions** Scientific investigations begin with *observation*, the act of noticing and describing events or processes in a careful, orderly way. Of course, scientific observation involves more than just "looking." The right kind of observation leads to asking questions that no one has asked (or answered) before.
>
> **Inferring and Forming a Hypothesis** After posing questions, scientists use further observations to make inferences. **An *inference* is a logical interpretation based on what scientists already know.** Inference, combined with a creative imagination, can lead to a hypothesis. A *hypothesis* is a tentative scientific explanation that can be tested by further observation or by experimentation.

**Two types of data** (p. 12)

> Scientists observe their experiments, gathering two main types of data. **Quantitative data are numbers obtained by counting or measuring.** In the marsh grass experiment, quantitative data could include the number of plants per plot; the length, width, and weight of each blade of grass; and so on. **Qualitative data are descriptive and involve characteristics that cannot usually be measured.** Qualitative data in the marsh grass experiment might include notes about whether the grass was growing upright or sideways, or whether one or another plot was disturbed by clam diggers.

**The salt marsh example** (Figure 1-2, p. 11). Researchers *observed* that marsh grass grows taller in some places than others. They *inferred* that something limits growth, and *hypothesized* that the limit is nitrogen. They added nitrogen to experimental plots, left control plots alone, and *collected data* on growth. The nitrogen plots grew taller, which supported the hypothesis.

**Scientific attitudes** (p. 16)

> **Curiosity, skepticism, open-mindedness, and creativity help scientists and engineers ask new questions and define new problems.**

**Community analysis and feedback** (p. 17)

> Scientists often collaborate in groups and communicate with other research groups. In order for research to be accepted, however, it must be officially shared with the scientific community.

Reviewers look carefully for mistakes, oversights, unfair influences, or fraud, in techniques and analysis. … **Peer review does not guarantee that a piece of work is correct, but it does certify that the work meets standards set by the scientific community.**

### Campbell Biology, Chapter 1, Concept 1.3

The teacher's *Observation and Inference* worksheet asks questions about this passage ("The scientific process begins with?", "Recorded observations are called?").

**Observation** (p. 16)

> Biology, like other sciences, begins with careful observations. In gathering information, biologists often use tools such as microscopes, precision thermometers, or high-speed cameras that extend their senses or facilitate careful measurement. … In exploring nature, biologists also rely heavily on the scientific literature, the published contributions of fellow scientists.

**Data** (p. 16)

> **Recorded observations are called data.** Put another way, data are items of information on which scientific inquiry is based. The term *data* implies numbers to many people. But some data are qualitative, often in the form of recorded descriptions rather than numerical measurements. For example, Jane Goodall spent decades recording her observations of chimpanzee behavior … Quantitative data are generally expressed as numerical measurements and often organized into tables and graphs.

**Inductive reasoning** (p. 17)

> Collecting and analyzing observations can lead to important conclusions based on a type of logic called inductive reasoning. **Through induction, we derive generalizations from a large number of specific observations.** "The sun always rises in the east" is one example. Another biological example is the generalization "All organisms are made of cells," which was based on two centuries of microscopic observations …

**Deductive reasoning** (p. 17)

> While induction entails reasoning from a set of specific observations to reach a general conclusion, **deductive reasoning involves logic that flows in the opposite direction, from the general to the specific.** … In the scientific process, deductions usually take the form of a prediction of results that will be found if a particular hypothesis (premise) is correct. … This deductive testing takes the form of "If . . . then" logic. In the case of the desk lamp example: If the burnt-out bulb hypothesis is correct, then the lamp should work if you replace the bulb with a new one.

**A hypothesis is never proved** (p. 17)

> Although a hypothesis can never be proved beyond all doubt, testing it in various ways can significantly increase our confidence in its validity.

## Part 2. Summary

**The big idea of 1A.** Science is a *skill*, not a list of facts, and skills improve through deliberate practice. Deliberate practice means: try it, get feedback, fix the gap, try again. That is the same loop scientists use, where they observe, explain, test, get feedback from peers, and revise. This first target teaches two sets of skills together:

<!-- figure: 1A-practice-loop -->


1. **How science thinks.**
   - Science begins with **observation**: noticing and describing carefully, using senses and instruments.
   - Recorded observations are **data**. **Quantitative** data are numbers (counts, measurements). **Qualitative** data are descriptions (color, behavior, shape).
   - From observations, scientists make **inferences**, logical interpretations based on what they already know. An inference plus creativity can become a testable **hypothesis**.
   - **Induction** moves from many specific observations to a general conclusion ("every swan I've seen is white → all swans are white"). Its conclusion is *probably* true, never guaranteed.
   - **Deduction** moves from a general statement to a specific prediction ("if nitrogen limits growth, then adding nitrogen should make grass taller"). If the premises are true, the conclusion must be true.
   - Science uses both. Induction builds the general idea, and deduction turns it into an if–then prediction you can test. Because the general idea came from induction, science never fully "proves" anything. Evidence makes an explanation more or less likely.
   - Science is a community effort: **collaboration**, **feedback**, and **peer review** catch mistakes and improve ideas.

2. **How this class works.**
   - The **KUDOS** and **Unit Guide** tell you what to learn: vocabulary, where to find information, and study questions.
   - **Formative** assessments (homework, practice tests, CFAs) are practice. They don't affect your grade and exist to give you feedback.
   - **Summative** assessments (quizzes, unit exams, final) measure what you learned and count toward your grade.
   - Academic skills you are expected to practice: note taking, reading comprehension, **annotation**, clear and concise writing, academic vocabulary, CER argumentation, and study and test-taking strategies.

**One-sentence version:** observe carefully, label what you *saw* separately from what you *think it means*, reason from evidence, and use feedback to keep improving, both in science and in this class.

## Part 3. Biology vocabulary

### annotation
- **Definition:** Notes you write directly on a text, diagram, or data table while reading. You mark key ideas, define unfamiliar words, ask questions, and make connections.
- **Source:** Biology Class Basics, slide 8 (listed as an academic skill). The textbook doesn't define it, though its Reading Tool boxes ask you to annotate.
- **What good annotation looks like:**
  - Underline or highlight the *one* key sentence in a paragraph, not the whole paragraph. (In Miller & Levine, the key sentence often starts with a small key icon.)
  - Write a short note in the margin in your own words: "inference = interpretation, not observation."
  - Put "?" next to anything confusing and write the question down.
  - Draw arrows to connect ideas: "→ same as the marsh grass example."
- **Why it matters:** It turns reading into active thinking, which is deliberate practice. You also remember more, and your notes make review faster before a test.
- **Common mistake:** highlighting everything. If everything is marked, nothing stands out.

<!-- figure: 1A-annotation-example -->


### formative (formative assessment)
- **Definition:** An assessment used *during* learning to check understanding and give feedback. It is practice, not a final score.
- **In this class:** homework, practice tests, and CFAs (common formative assessments). According to the Class Basics slides, formative assessments **do not affect your grade** and are a "chance for feedback and improvement."
- **Memory hook:** *form*ative helps *form* your learning, like clay still being shaped.
- **How to use it:** treat each formative as information. Every mistake tells you exactly what to practice before the real test.

### summative (summative assessment)
- **Definition:** An assessment at the *end* of a period of learning that measures what you learned and counts toward your grade.
- **In this class:** quizzes, unit exams, and the final exam. Quizzes/exams are 45% of the grade and the final is 20%.
- **Memory hook:** *sum*mative is the *sum* of what you learned.
- **Formative vs. summative:** the same kind of question can appear on both. What differs is the *purpose*: to practice and get feedback (formative) or to measure and grade (summative).

<!-- figure: 1A-formative-summative -->


### quantitative / qualitative (data)
- **Quantitative data:** "numbers obtained by counting or measuring" (M&L p. 12). Examples: number of plants per plot, blade length in cm, temperature in °C, a heart rate of 72 beats per minute.
- **Qualitative data:** "descriptive and involve characteristics that cannot usually be measured" (M&L p. 12). Examples: grass growing upright or sideways, leaf color is yellow, the chimp groomed another chimp (Jane Goodall's notes, Campbell p. 16).
- **Memory hook:** *quanti*tative = *quanti*ty (how many, how much); *quali*tative = *quali*ty (what kind, what it's like).
- **Same object, both kinds:** a microscope can count blood cells (quantitative) or show the steps of cell division (qualitative). The worksheet uses this example.
- **Watch out:** a description with a number-like word is still qualitative if nothing was counted or measured. "Many plants" is qualitative; "47 plants" is quantitative.

<!-- bookfig: c-goodall -->


### data
- **Definition:** "Recorded observations are called data" (Campbell p. 16). Data are the information scientific inquiry is based on, collected with senses or instruments.
- **Two types:** quantitative and qualitative (above).
- **Role in science:** data *support or reject* a hypothesis (M&L p. 8). Scientists often organize data in tables and graphs and use statistics to check whether a result is real or just random chance (M&L p. 13; Campbell p. 16).
- **Grammar note:** *data* is plural (the singular is *datum*), so scientists write "the data show …"
- **Good data are:** recorded carefully, repeated (replicated), and taken from a large enough sample. The larger the sample size, the more reliable the analysis (M&L p. 13).

<!-- bookfig: c-mice-data -->




### inference
- **Definition:** "a logical interpretation based on what scientists already know" (M&L p. 12). An inference goes *beyond* what was directly observed to explain it.
- **Observation vs. inference:**

  | Observation (what you detect) | Inference (what you conclude it means) |
  |---|---|
  | The grass in location A is 90 cm tall; in location B it is 30 cm. | Something in location B is limiting growth. |
  | The road signs are in Portuguese. (Geotastic) | This is probably Brazil or Portugal. |
  | There is a tan line on the man's wrist, but not his hand. (Sherlock) | He has recently been somewhere sunny. |
  | The lamp won't light. | The bulb is probably burnt out. |

- **Key point:** an inference can be wrong even when the observation is right. That's why scientists turn inferences into hypotheses and test them.
- **Test tip:** if a statement contains "because," "probably," "must be," or a cause, it's usually an inference.

## Part 4. Academic vocabulary

These are general academic words, not just biology words. You'll see them on tests and in instructions.

### assess / assessment
- **Meaning:** to *assess* is to judge or measure the quality, amount, or level of something. An *assessment* is the tool or event that does the judging: a test, quiz, checkup, or lab report.
- **In class:** formative and summative are the two kinds of assessment.
- **Example sentence:** "The teacher used a checkup to *assess* whether students completed the homework."
- **In science:** scientists assess evidence to decide whether it supports a hypothesis. Peer reviewers assess each other's papers.

### collaboration
- **Meaning:** working together with others toward a shared goal. (Verb: *collaborate*; adjective: *collaborative*.)
- **In science:** "Scientists often collaborate in groups and communicate with other research groups" (M&L p. 17). Discussing with colleagues, sharing data, replication, and peer review are all forms of collaboration (M&L Figure 1-6).
- **In class:** lab partners, group discussion, sharing class data in a Google Sheet (Geotastic).
- **Caution:** collaboration means building understanding together, not copying. You still need to be able to explain the idea on your own.

<!-- bookfig: c-community -->


### commitment
- **Meaning:** a promise or dedication to keep doing something, especially when it's hard. (Verb: *commit*.)
- **In class:** 15–30 minutes of *focused* homework per night (Class Basics slide 7), coming to tutorial when stuck, and doing formative practice even though it isn't graded.
- **In science:** Jane Goodall's decades of observing chimpanzees (Campbell p. 16), and Darwin spending many years assembling his hypotheses into a theory (M&L p. 14).
- **Link to 1A:** deliberate practice only works with commitment, meaning repeated, focused effort over time.

### effective
- **Meaning:** successful in producing the intended result. (Noun: *effectiveness*.)
- **Don't confuse:** *effective* (it works) and *efficient* (it works without wasting time or energy). Rereading notes for an hour can be *inefficient* and *ineffective*. Quizzing yourself for 20 minutes is usually more effective.
- **Example sentence:** "Annotating the reading was an *effective* way to prepare for the quiz."
- **In science:** a controlled experiment is an effective design because changing only one variable lets you tell which variable caused the result (M&L p. 12).

### feedback
- **Meaning:** information about your performance that tells you what is working and what to improve.
- **In class:** formative assessments are a "chance for feedback and improvement" (Class Basics slide 6). Comments on assignments and retake opportunities are also feedback.
- **In science:** "Community Analysis and Feedback" (M&L p. 17). Peer reviewers give feedback that catches "mistakes, oversights, unfair influences, or fraud" (M&L p. 18).
- **How to use it:** (1) read the feedback, (2) find the specific gap, (3) practice that exact skill, (4) check again. That loop *is* deliberate practice.
- **Heads-up for 1B:** in biology, *feedback* also means something different: a *feedback loop* in the body (negative and positive feedback) that keeps conditions stable. Same word, related idea: output information is used to adjust the next step.

### induction / deduction
- **Induction (inductive reasoning):** reasoning from **specific observations → a general conclusion**. Campbell p. 17: "we derive generalizations from a large number of specific observations."
  - Example: "Every time I drop an apple it falls. → All dropped apples will fall." (teacher's slide 5)
  - Example: "Two centuries of microscope observations found cells in every organism → All organisms are made of cells." (Campbell p. 17)
  - The conclusion is only *probably* true. New evidence could overturn it.
- **Deduction (deductive reasoning):** reasoning from **general premises → a specific conclusion**. Campbell p. 17: "from the general to the specific."
  - Example: "All antibiotics kill bacteria. Penicillin is an antibiotic. Therefore penicillin kills bacteria." (teacher's slide 5)
  - If the premises are true and the logic is valid, the conclusion **must** be true. It can only be false if a premise is false.
  - In science, deduction takes the form of an **if … then** prediction.
- **Memory hook:** **In**duction = going **in**to a big idea from small pieces (specific → general). **De**duction = going **down** from the big idea to one case (general → specific).

<!-- figure: 1A-induction-deduction -->

- **Quick check** (teacher's slide 5):
  - "All dolphins are mammals. All mammals have kidneys. Dolphins have kidneys." → *deductive*
  - "Chimps and humans share 99% of their genetic material. Chimps and humans evolved from a common ancestor." → *inductive*
  - "5, 8, 11, 14, 17, __. The next number is 20." → *inductive* (a pattern is assumed to continue)

### observation
- **Meaning:** "the act of noticing and describing events or processes in a careful, orderly way" (M&L p. 12). Also the thing noticed ("my observation was …").
- **How we observe:** with the five senses, extended by instruments such as microscopes, thermometers, high-speed cameras, rulers, scales, and pH meters (Campbell p. 16). Scientists also rely on others' published observations, the scientific literature (Campbell p. 16).
- **Good observations are:** specific, factual, and free of interpretation. "The leaf is yellow with brown edges" is an observation. "The leaf is dying" is an inference.
- **Why it matters:** observation starts every investigation, and "the right kind of observation leads to asking questions that no one has asked (or answered) before" (M&L p. 12).

### Q3. How are observation and inference related to science?

**Short answer:** Science begins with observation. Inferences are the explanations scientists build from those observations. A good inference becomes a testable hypothesis, and new observations then test it. Science is a cycle of observing and inferring.

**In detail:**

1. **Observation comes first.** "Scientific investigations begin with observation, the act of noticing and describing events or processes in a careful, orderly way" (M&L p. 12). Scientists observe with their senses and with instruments that extend their senses, like microscopes and thermometers (Campbell p. 16). Recorded observations are **data** (Campbell p. 16), either quantitative or qualitative.

2. **Inference interprets the observation.** "An inference is a logical interpretation based on what scientists already know" (M&L p. 12). An observation tells you *what*. An inference proposes *why* or *what it means*. Inferences use prior knowledge, so the same observation can lead different people to different inferences.

3. **Inference leads to a hypothesis.** "Inference, combined with a creative imagination, can lead to a hypothesis," a tentative explanation that can be tested (M&L p. 12).

4. **More observations test it.** The hypothesis predicts what *should* be observed if it's true. Scientists collect new data through experiments or field observations. The data support or reject the inference.


<!-- figure: 1A-obs-inf-chain -->

**Worked example: the salt marsh (M&L Figure 1-2, p. 11)**

| Step | What happened |
|--------|--------------------|
| Observation | Marsh grass grows taller in some places than others. |
| Inference | Something is limiting growth in the short-grass places. |
| Hypothesis | Marsh grass growth is limited by available nitrogen. |
| Test (new observations) | Add nitrogen to experimental plots, none to control plots, and measure growth. |
| Result | Nitrogen plots grew taller → hypothesis supported. |

**Worked example: Sherlock Holmes (class worksheet).** Sherlock notices small details, such as a tan line, a worn sleeve, or mud on a shoe. Those are **observations**, and most of them are **qualitative** (descriptions, not measurements). From them he draws conclusions about where someone has been or what their job is. Those are **inferences**. He is effective because his observations are unusually careful and his inferences use a lot of background knowledge. But an inference can be wrong even if the observation is right. That's why science, unlike a detective story, *tests* inferences.

**Worked example: Geotastic (class activity).** In the game, the clues you see (language on signs, which side of the road cars drive on, vegetation) are observations. Your guess of the location is an inference. The class prediction was that more "advances" (more observations) would bring guesses closer to the real location, meaning a smaller distance in km. That is the scientific idea that **more and better observations lead to more accurate inferences.**

**Why the distinction matters in science:**
- Mixing up observation and inference leads to bad conclusions. You might treat a guess as a fact.
- When you write a CER, your **evidence** must be observations/data, and your **claim** is an inference. Keeping them separate is what makes the argument convincing.

### Q4. What is the difference between induction and deduction? How does this relate to science?

**The difference**

| | Induction (inductive reasoning) | Deduction (deductive reasoning) |
|--------|------------------|------------------|
| Direction | Specific → general | General → specific |
| Starts with | Evidence, data, observations | General rules or premises |
| Ends with | A general rule or pattern that explains the evidence | A specific conclusion or prediction |
| Certainty | **Not guaranteed.** Only *probably* true, based on past experience. | **Guaranteed** *if* the premises are true and the logic is valid. |
| Teacher's example | "The sun has always risen. Therefore, the sun will rise tomorrow." | "All dogs have four legs. My pet is a dog. Therefore, my pet has four legs." |
| Campbell's example | "All organisms are made of cells" (from two centuries of observations) | "If the burnt-out bulb hypothesis is correct, then the lamp should work if you replace the bulb." |

- **Induction** (Campbell p. 17): "we derive generalizations from a large number of specific observations." It assumes the past predicts the future, and that isn't always true. So an inductive conclusion is only "probabilistically true based on past experience" (teacher's slide 4).
- **Deduction** (Campbell p. 17): "logic that flows in the opposite direction, from the general to the specific." The conclusion can only be false if one of the premises is false (teacher's slide 3).

**How it relates to science: science uses both, in a cycle.**

1. **Induction builds hypotheses and theories.** Scientists observe many specific cases and look for a pattern. The hypothesis is "a rational accounting for a set of observations, based on the available data and guided by inductive reasoning" (Campbell p. 17).
2. **Deduction makes testable predictions.** From the hypothesis, scientists reason: *if* the hypothesis is true, *then* this specific result should happen. "This deductive testing takes the form of 'If . . . then' logic" (Campbell p. 17). Example: *If* nitrogen limits marsh grass growth, *then* grass given extra nitrogen should grow taller than grass without it.
3. **Experiments check the prediction.** If the result matches, the hypothesis is supported. If not, it's rejected or revised. Then the cycle repeats.


<!-- bookfig: c-desklamp -->

**Why science never "proves" anything.** The teacher's slide 6 says science "is not a deductive process, it is an inductive one." The general ideas science rests on come from induction, so they can never be 100% certain:
- More supporting data *increases the probability* that a theory is true, but never makes it certain.
- New data that goes against the pattern can reduce that probability.
- Someone might find a pattern or theory that explains the existing evidence better.

The textbooks say the same thing: "science rarely 'proves' anything" (M&L p. 10), and "a hypothesis can never be proved beyond all doubt" (Campbell p. 17). Confidence grows as a hypothesis survives more tests, and this is how scientific consensus forms.

**A connection to the Sherlock worksheet (Q16 on that sheet).** Sherlock is called the "Master of Deduction," but most of what he does is **induction**. He starts from specific observations (a tan line, a limp) and reaches a general conclusion ("you've been in Afghanistan"). His conclusions are very probable, not guaranteed. So a strong answer is: *No, not really. He mostly reasons from specific observations to a general conclusion, which is inductive reasoning. His conclusions could be wrong if another explanation fits the same clues.* (Arguing the other way is possible when he applies a general rule to a specific case, for example "people who have been in the tropics are tanned; this man is tanned …". Either way, name the direction of reasoning and use a specific example from the clip.)
