## Section I. Multiple choice

Choose the **one** best answer for each question.

### Questions 1–3 refer to the characteristics of life.

**1.** A student claims that fire is alive because it grows, uses oxygen, and releases energy. Which fact provides the **strongest** evidence against this claim?

(A) Fire gives off light.
(B) Fire is not made of cells and has no genetic code.
(C) Fire can move from place to place.
(D) Fire needs fuel to keep burning.

**2.** A mule is the sterile offspring of a horse and a donkey. It cannot reproduce. Why do biologists still classify a mule as a living organism?

(A) Mules are large animals.
(B) The characteristics of life describe living things in general; a mule is made of cells, uses energy, maintains homeostasis, and has DNA.
(C) Any organism that can move is alive.
(D) Mules will evolve the ability to reproduce during their lifetimes.

**3.** Which characteristic of life do viruses show **only** when they are inside a host cell?

(A) Being made of cells
(B) Maintaining homeostasis
(C) Reproducing
(D) Carrying out their own metabolism

**Scoring guide.** 14–16 correct: ready. 11–13: review the explanations for the ones you missed. 10 or fewer: reread the study guide's Parts 2–3, then retake.

### Section I explanations

**1. (B)**
*Skill: apply the criteria for life (M&L p. 22).*
Living things are made of **cells** and have a **universal genetic code**. Fire has neither. A single missing criterion rules something out, so (B) is the strongest evidence.
- (A), (C), (D): these describe things fire *does*, so they don't argue against the claim. Some living things also give off light, move, or need fuel.

**2. (B)**
*Skill: the criteria describe life in general.*
A mule meets every other criterion, and it came from parents that reproduced. The criteria describe living things as a group, not every individual.
- (A): size isn't a criterion.
- (C): movement alone isn't enough (cars and clouds move).
- (D): individuals don't evolve; groups do, over generations.

**3. (C) Reproducing**
*Skill: why viruses are borderline.*
Viruses "can reproduce" only "after infecting living things" (M&L p. 24): they use the host cell's machinery.
- (A), (B), (D): viruses never have cells, homeostasis, or their own metabolism, even inside a host.

**4. (C)**
*Skill: two directions of metabolism (Campbell p. 146).*
Joining amino acids into a protein **builds** a large molecule and **uses** energy: an anabolic (building-up) reaction.
- (A), (B), (D): all break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy (catabolic).

**5. (B)**
*Skill: interpret a graph with error bars.*
CO₂ rose from 10 to 30 °C, was similar at 30 and 40 °C (the error bars overlap), and **crashed at 50 °C**. High heat damages the enzymes that carry out metabolism.
- (A): the 50 °C bar is lowest, not highest.
- (C): the 30 and 40 °C error bars overlap, so the data don't show a clear difference.
- (D): there is a large effect.

**6. (B)**
*Skill: describe a trend in two variables.*
Temperature rose from 37.1 °C at 10 minutes, the sweat rate climbed at 20–30 minutes, and temperature **leveled off** at 38.1 °C instead of continuing up. After exercise both returned toward normal.
- (A): temperature leveled off.
- (C): sweat rate was low (0.1–0.2 L/h) before exercise.
- (D): temperature rose during exercise, although sweating limited the rise.

**7. (B)**
*Skill: identify negative feedback.*
The response (sweating) **opposes** the change (rising temperature). That is negative feedback, "a stimulus produces a response that opposes the original stimulus" (M&L p. 907).
- (A), (C): positive feedback would make temperature rise faster and faster.
- (D): a changing value doesn't mean there's no feedback. Feedback responds *to* change.

**8. (C) The hypothalamus**
*Skill: parts of a feedback loop.*
"A part of the brain called the hypothalamus contains nerve cells that monitor body temperature" (M&L p. 908), and it sends the signals that start sweating.
- (A): sweat glands are **effectors**: they carry out the response.
- (B): skeletal muscles are effectors for shivering.
- (D): the skin is where cooling happens, not where the decision is made.

**9. (A)**
*Skill: homeostasis as a dynamic equilibrium.*
"Physiological responses to stimuli are not instantaneous … homeostasis moderates but doesn't eliminate changes" (Campbell p. 894).
- (B): sweating cools the body.
- (C): temperature returned to 37.1 °C after exercise, so the set point didn't change permanently.
- (D): feedback is most important during challenges like exercise.

**10. (A)**
*Skill: read a line graph.*
Person X peaked at 150 mg/100 mL at 30 minutes and was back to 100 (inside the normal band) by 90 minutes.
- (B): glucose clearly changed.
- (C): it fell after 30 minutes.
- (D): it stayed at or above 84, inside the normal range.

**11. (B)**
*Skill: propose an explanation from data.*
Person Y started high (120), rose higher (235), and stayed far above the normal range for 3 hours. Glucose isn't being removed, which is what insulin does. This pattern is typical of diabetes.
- (A): extra glucagon would raise glucose, but the main problem shown is failure to *lower* it after the drink; (B) explains that more directly.
- (C): Person Y's regulation is clearly weaker.
- (D): the question says both drank the same solution.

**12. (B)**
*Skill: the opposing hormone.*
When glucose drops, "the pancreas releases glucagon. Glucagon stimulates liver and skeletal muscle cells to break down glycogen and release glucose into the blood" (M&L p. 932).
- (A), (C): these lower blood glucose.
- (D): shivering produces heat, not glucose.

**13. (C)**
*Skill: identify positive feedback.*
In childbirth, each contraction causes more oxytocin, which causes stronger contractions. The response **amplifies** the stimulus (Campbell p. 894).
- (A), (B), (D): each response opposes a change, which is negative feedback.

**14. (B)**
*Skill: feedback in a non-living system (teacher's slide 7).*
Warming → less ice → darker surface → more heat absorbed → more warming. The result **reinforces** the original change.
- (A): the loop amplifies warming; it doesn't oppose it.
- (C): nothing is being kept stable.
- (D): feedback loops occur in any system, including Earth's climate.

**15. (C)**
*Skill: evaluate a model.*
Every model leaves something out. The body uses many sensors and several responses (sweating, shivering, changing blood flow to the skin), while a simple thermostat switches one heater.
- (A), (B), (D): these are *strengths* of the model: ways it matches the body.

**16. (B)**
*Skill: design a controlled experiment.*
In (B) the only difference between groups is the drink (the independent variable); exercise, room, time, amount drunk, and measuring method are all controlled.
- (A): three things differ (drink, temperature, and type of exercise).
- (C): one person per group is too small a sample, and "feels better" isn't measurable.
- (D): no comparison group.

### Section II scoring guides and model answers

#### Question 1 (10 points)

| Part | Points | Earn the point(s) for … |
|---|---|--------------------|
| (a) | 1 | Independent: temperature. Dependent: volume of CO₂ released. Controlled: any one (mass of yeast, volume or concentration of sugar water, flask size, time). All three needed for the point. |
| (b) | 1 | Yeast release CO₂ when they break down sugar in cellular respiration, a metabolic reaction, so more CO₂ means faster metabolism. |
| (c) | 2 | Correct labeled axes with units: x = time (min), y = CO₂ released (mL) (1). Three correctly plotted lines with a key (1). |
| (d) | 2 | 20 °C: 5.8 ÷ 20 = **0.29 mL/min** (1). 30 °C: 12.2 ÷ 20 = **0.61 mL/min** (1). |
| (e) | 1 | Raising the temperature from 20 °C to 30 °C about doubled the rate; 40 °C (0.65 mL/min) was only slightly faster than 30 °C. |
| (f) | 2 | The prediction is not supported (1). Reasoning: very high temperatures damage the enzymes and other proteins that carry out metabolism, so the rate would likely drop sharply, as it did at 50 °C in the multiple-choice data (1). |
| (g) | 1 | Flasks with sugar water but no yeast, kept at each temperature. They should release no CO₂. |

**Model answer.**
(a) The independent variable is the temperature of the water bath. The dependent variable is the volume of CO₂ released. The mass of yeast should be kept the same in every flask.
(b) Yeast break down sugar in cellular respiration and release CO₂ as a product. Cellular respiration is part of metabolism, so the faster CO₂ is released, the faster the yeast's metabolism.
(c) *A line graph: x-axis "Time (min)" from 0 to 20; y-axis "CO₂ released (mL)" from 0 to at least 13; three lines (20, 30, 40 °C) with a key. The 30 and 40 °C lines are close together and much steeper than the 20 °C line.*
(d) 20 °C: 5.8 mL ÷ 20 min = 0.29 mL/min. 30 °C: 12.2 mL ÷ 20 min = 0.61 mL/min.
(e) Warmer temperatures sped up metabolism: going from 20 °C to 30 °C roughly doubled the rate (0.29 → 0.61 mL/min). Going from 30 °C to 40 °C changed it only a little (0.61 → 0.65 mL/min), so the rate levels off.
(f) I disagree. Metabolism depends on enzymes, which are proteins that work best within a certain temperature range. At 60 °C the heat would likely damage the enzymes (and kill the yeast), so CO₂ release would drop sharply instead of increasing.
(g) Set up flasks with sugar water but no yeast at each temperature. If they release no CO₂, the CO₂ in the other flasks must come from the yeast.

#### Question 2 (4 points)

| Part | Earn 1 point for … |
|---|--------------------|
| (a) | Two of: made of a cell (has a cell membrane); responds to the environment or moves; reproduces (numbers increase). |
| (b) | A valid test for another criterion, e.g., check for DNA, measure use of food or oxygen (metabolism), show a response to a stimulus such as light, or watch it grow. |
| (c) | Nonliving things can share single traits with living things (fire grows and uses energy; cars move), so all the criteria are needed together. |
| (d) | Viruses reproduce and evolve (and have genetic material) but only inside a host cell; they have no cells and no metabolism of their own. |

**Model answer.**
(a) It seems to be made of a cell (it has a cell membrane), and it reproduces (the number increases over several days). Its movement may also be a response to its environment.
(b) I could test whether it contains DNA, or put a light at one side of the sample to see if the objects move toward or away from it (a response to a stimulus). Seeing each object take in food and grow would show metabolism and growth.
(c) Many nonliving things have one or two traits of life: fire grows and uses energy, and a car moves and responds to its driver. Only living things have all of the characteristics together, so one trait alone can't prove something is alive.
(d) Viruses have genetic material and can reproduce and evolve, but only after infecting a living cell and using its machinery. They are not made of cells and have no metabolism of their own, so they meet some criteria but not others.

#### Question 3 (4 points)

| Part | Earn 1 point for … |
|---|--------------------|
| (a) | A rise in blood glucose (for example, after eating). |
| (b) | Insulin causes body cells to take up glucose and the liver to store it as glycogen, lowering blood glucose. |
| (c) | The response (lowering glucose) opposes the stimulus (high glucose), bringing the level back toward the set point. |
| (d) | Blood glucose would rise after a meal and stay high, because cells wouldn't take up glucose even with insulin present. |

**Model answer.**
(a) An increase in blood glucose, such as after eating a meal.
(b) Insulin travels in the blood and signals body cells to take up glucose and the liver to store it as glycogen. Glucose leaves the blood, so the level falls back to the normal range (70–110 mg/100 mL).
(c) The stimulus is high blood glucose, and the response (insulin causing glucose uptake) lowers it. The response opposes the change, so it is negative feedback. Once glucose is normal, insulin release decreases.
(d) Blood glucose would rise after the meal and stay high for a long time (like Person Y in Questions 10–11). Even if the pancreas released insulin, the cells wouldn't respond by taking up glucose, so glucose would stay in the blood.

#### Question 4 (4 points)

| Part | Earn 1 point for … |
|---|--------------------|
| (a) | Keeping internal conditions relatively constant (within a range) despite changes in the internal or external environment. |
| (b) | Conditions constantly change a little and are constantly corrected, so they stay within a range; a correct body-temperature example. |
| (c) | Negative feedback opposes a change and stabilizes the system; positive feedback amplifies a change and pushes the system further from its starting point. |
| (d) | Childbirth is a process that must be completed; positive feedback builds contractions until the baby is born, then the loop stops. |

**Model answer.**
(a) Homeostasis is the maintenance of relatively constant internal conditions, such as temperature, water, and blood glucose, within a normal range, even when the internal or external environment changes.
(b) Dynamic equilibrium means the system is balanced overall but always moving and adjusting. Body temperature rises a little during exercise and falls a little on a cold day, but sweating and shivering keep correcting it, so it stays around 36–38 °C.
(c) Negative feedback responds to a change by pushing the condition back the opposite way, which keeps the system stable. Positive feedback responds to a change by pushing the condition further in the same direction, so the change gets bigger and bigger.
(d) Childbirth needs to *finish* rather than stay stable. Each contraction triggers the release of more oxytocin, which causes stronger contractions, until the baby is born. Then the stimulus is gone and the loop ends. Positive feedback drives the process to completion quickly.
