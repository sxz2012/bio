### Miller & Levine, Chapter 27 (homeostasis and feedback)

**Homeostasis** (p. 907)

> This process is called homeostasis, which means "keeping things the same." **Homeostasis describes the relatively constant internal conditions that organisms maintain despite changes in internal and external environments.** Homeostasis may not be obvious, but for a living organism, it's a matter of life or death.

The margin note on the same page: *"Root Words The root word stasis means 'a state of balance or equilibrium.'"*

**A nonliving model: the thermostat** (p. 907)

> In many homes, heat is supplied by a furnace. When the temperature within the house drops below a set point, a thermostat sensor switches the furnace on. Heat produced by the furnace warms the house. When the temperature rises above the set point, the thermostat switches the furnace off, keeping the temperature within a narrow range.
>
> A system like this example is controlled by feedback inhibition. **Feedback inhibition, or negative feedback, is the process in which a stimulus produces a response that opposes the original stimulus.** … Systems controlled by feedback inhibition are generally very stable.

**A living example: body temperature** (p. 908)

> If your body temperature drops well below its normal range, the hypothalamus releases chemicals that signal muscles just below the surface of the skin to contract involuntarily—to "shiver." These muscle contractions release heat, which helps the body temperature to rise.
>
> If body temperature rises too far above 37°C, the hypothalamus slows down cellular activities to reduce heat production. … The body also responds to high temperatures by producing sweat, which helps to cool the body surface by evaporation.

**Blood glucose** (p. 932)

> Glucose concentration in the bloodstream is controlled by insulin and glucagon. When blood glucose concentration rises, the pancreas releases insulin. … In between meals, when blood glucose concentration drops, the pancreas releases glucagon. Glucagon stimulates liver and skeletal muscle cells to break down glycogen and release glucose into the blood. These actions raise blood glucose concentrations back to normal.

### Campbell Biology

**Properties of life** (Ch. 1, p. 3). Campbell says life "defies a simple definition. We recognize life by what living things do." Its Figure 1.2 lists seven properties: **order, evolutionary adaptation, regulation, reproduction, energy processing, growth and development, and response to the environment.**

**Feedback regulation** (Ch. 1, p. 9)

> **In feedback regulation, the output or product of a process regulates that very process.** The most common form of regulation in living systems is negative feedback, a loop in which the response acts in opposition to the initial stimulus. … Though less common than processes regulated by negative feedback, there are also many biological processes regulated by **positive feedback, in which an end product reinforces or strengthens the stimulus.** The clotting of your blood in response to injury is an example.

**Metabolism** (Ch. 8, p. 146)

> **The totality of an organism's chemical reactions is called metabolism** (from the Greek *metabole*, change). … Some metabolic pathways release energy by breaking down complex molecules to simpler compounds. These degradative processes are called catabolic pathways, or breakdown pathways. … Anabolic pathways, in contrast, consume energy to build complicated molecules from simpler ones; they are sometimes called biosynthetic pathways.

**Homeostasis and the normal range** (Ch. 40, p. 893)

> In achieving homeostasis, animals maintain a "steady state"—a relatively constant internal environment—even when the external environment changes significantly. … For example, humans maintain a fairly constant body temperature of between approximately 36° and 38°C (97°–99°F), a blood pH within 0.1 pH unit of 7.4, and a blood glucose concentration that is predominantly in the range of 70–110 mg of glucose per 100 mL of blood.

**Dynamic equilibrium and positive feedback** (Ch. 40, p. 894)

> **Homeostasis is a dynamic equilibrium, an interplay between external factors that tend to change the internal environment and internal control mechanisms that oppose such changes.** … Unlike negative feedback, positive feedback is a control mechanism that amplifies the stimulus. In animals, positive-feedback loops do not play a major role in homeostasis, but instead help drive processes to completion. During childbirth, for instance, the pressure of the baby's head against sensors near the opening of the mother's uterus stimulates the uterus to contract.

## Part 2. Summary

**The big idea of 1B.** Scientists answer "What is life?" and "How do living things stay alive?" with evidence from many methods: observation, controlled experiments, and models. They keep revising their explanations when new evidence or better arguments appear. Two ideas sit at the center:

**1. What counts as alive.** No single trait defines life. Fire moves and gives off light, but it isn't alive. Instead, biologists use a *set of criteria*. According to Miller & Levine, living things:
1. are made of **cells**
2. **reproduce** (sexually or asexually)
3. are based on a universal **genetic code** (DNA)
4. **grow and develop**
5. **obtain and use materials and energy** (metabolism)
6. **respond to their environment** (stimuli)
7. **maintain a stable internal environment** (homeostasis)
8. **evolve**, changing over time as a group

Something is considered alive when it shows *all* of these, not just one or two. **Viruses** are the classic borderline case: they reproduce and evolve, but only inside a host cell, and they aren't made of cells. Whether to call them alive is a real scientific argument, which is exactly what the 1B target statement describes.

**2. How living things stay stable.**
- **Metabolism** is all the chemical reactions in an organism. Some build molecules up (photosynthesis, protein synthesis), and some break them down (cellular respiration, digestion).
- **Homeostasis** is keeping internal conditions (temperature, water, blood sugar, pH) within a safe **range** even when the outside world changes. It is a **dynamic equilibrium**: conditions constantly drift and get corrected, rather than staying perfectly fixed.
- Organisms maintain homeostasis through **feedback loops**, where a change in a condition triggers a response that affects that same condition.
  - **Negative feedback** *opposes* the change, which keeps the system stable. Examples: sweating and shivering for body temperature, insulin and glucagon for blood sugar. This is how homeostasis works.
  - **Positive feedback** *amplifies* the change, pushing the system further in the same direction until something ends it. Examples: childbirth contractions, blood clotting, the melting of ice speeding up global warming. It does *not* maintain homeostasis, and in nature it's often harmful.

**One-sentence version:** life is recognized by a set of shared characteristics, and one of the most important is homeostasis, which organisms maintain mostly through negative feedback loops that keep conditions within a stable range.

## Part 3. Biology vocabulary

### organism
- **Definition:** an individual living thing. "Living things, or organisms, are made up of small, self-contained units called cells" (M&L p. 23).
- **Unicellular vs. multicellular** (from the What Is Life? notes): a unicellular organism is a single cell (bacteria, amoeba). A multicellular organism is made of many cells (a tree, a human).
- **Examples:** a bacterium, a mushroom, a sunflower, a person. **Non-examples:** a virus (borderline, usually not counted), a cell taken out of your body (alive, but not an organism by itself), a fire.
- **Link to ecology:** organisms are the smallest level in ecology. Organisms → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere.

<!-- bookfig: c-levels -->


### metabolism
- **Definition:** "The combination of chemical reactions [an organism uses] as it carries out its life processes" (M&L p. 24). Campbell (p. 146): "The totality of an organism's chemical reactions." The teacher's definition: "All of the chemical reactions that build up and break down molecules in living things."
- **Two directions:**

  | Building up (anabolic) | Breaking down (catabolic) |
  |---|---|
  | uses energy | releases energy |
  | small molecules → big molecules | big molecules → small molecules |
  | photosynthesis (CO₂ + H₂O → glucose) | cellular respiration (glucose → CO₂ + H₂O + energy) |
  | protein synthesis (amino acids → protein) | digestion (starch → sugars) |

- **Memory hook:** *meta* + *bol* comes from the Greek *metabole*, "change" (Campbell p. 146). Metabolism is all the chemical *changes* in your body.
- **Why it matters for 1C:** photosynthesis and cellular respiration are how energy and matter move through ecosystems.

<!-- figure: 1B-metabolism -->


### homeostasis
- **Definition:** "the relatively constant internal conditions that organisms maintain despite changes in internal and external environments" (M&L p. 907). The teacher's note: "Organisms use feedback mechanisms to maintain internal conditions within certain limits, even as external conditions change."
- **Word parts:** *homeo-* = same, *-stasis* = "a state of balance or equilibrium" (M&L p. 907). Together: "keeping things the same."
- **What gets kept stable in humans** (Campbell p. 893):
  - body temperature: about 36–38 °C (97–99 °F)
  - blood pH: within 0.1 of 7.4
  - blood glucose: mostly 70–110 mg per 100 mL
  - water balance
- **Key point:** homeostasis doesn't mean *no* change. The value moves up and down within a range, which is why it's called a **dynamic equilibrium** (Campbell p. 894; teacher's slide 2).
- **Why it matters:** "any breakdown of homeostasis may have serious or even fatal consequences" (M&L p. 25). Heatstroke, hypothermia, dehydration, and uncontrolled diabetes are all failures of homeostasis.

<!-- figure: 1B-dynamic-eq -->


### stable / stability
- **Definition:** *stable* means staying about the same and resisting change. *Stability* is the quality of being stable.
- **In biology:** a stable internal environment is one of the characteristics of life. "Systems controlled by feedback inhibition are generally very stable" (M&L p. 907).
- **Stable ≠ unchanging:** a stable body temperature still rises a little during exercise and falls a little at night. It just returns toward the normal value.
- **Crosscutting concept:** "Stability and Change" is one of the big themes of biology (M&L p. 25). Later in this unit you'll see it again: stable ecosystems (2A) and stable populations (2C).

### feedback loop
- **Definition:** "any mechanism in which a condition triggers some action that causes a change in that same condition" (teacher's slide 2). Campbell (p. 9): "the output or product of a process regulates that very process."
- **Why it's called a loop:** the result circles back to affect the starting point.

  **condition changes → sensor detects it → control center → response → the condition changes back** (and the loop starts again)

- **Parts** (Campbell pp. 893–894; M&L p. 907):
  - **Stimulus / condition:** the thing being regulated, such as body temperature
  - **Sensor:** detects the change, such as nerve cells in the hypothalamus or a thermostat
  - **Set point:** the target value, such as 37 °C
  - **Effector / response:** what acts to change the condition, such as sweat glands, muscles (shivering), or a furnace
- **Two types:** negative and positive (below).

<!-- bookfig: c-40-8 -->


### negative feedback
- **Definition:** "the process in which a stimulus produces a response that opposes the original stimulus" (M&L p. 907). Also called **feedback inhibition**.
- **What it does:** it *reverses* a change and brings the condition back toward the set point. It keeps things **stable**, so it's the main way organisms maintain homeostasis. It is "the most common form of regulation in living systems" (Campbell p. 9).
- **Examples:**
  - **Body temperature** (teacher's slide 4; M&L p. 908): too hot → sweat → cools down. Too cold → shiver → warms up.
  - **Blood glucose** (M&L p. 932; Campbell p. 9): high after a meal → pancreas releases insulin → cells take up and store glucose → level falls. Low between meals → glucagon → liver releases glucose → level rises.
  - **Water balance** (M&L pp. 24, 932): water is low → you feel thirsty and the kidneys save water (ADH) → water returns to normal.
  - **Thermostat** (M&L p. 907): too cold → furnace on → warms → furnace off.
  - **Climate** (teacher's slide 9): Earth warms → more evaporation → more clouds → more sunlight reflected → cooling.
- **Memory hook:** negative = *negates* (cancels) the change.

<!-- bookfig: c-glucose-fb -->


### positive feedback
- **Definition:** "an end product reinforces or strengthens the stimulus" (Campbell p. 9). "A control mechanism that amplifies the stimulus" (Campbell p. 894).
- **What it does:** it pushes the condition *further in the same direction*, so the change keeps growing until something outside the loop stops it. It does **not** maintain homeostasis. In animals it helps "drive processes to completion" (Campbell p. 894). In natural systems it is often harmful (teacher's slide 3).
- **Examples:**
  - **Childbirth** (teacher's slide 5; Campbell p. 894): baby's head presses on the cervix → nerve signals to the brain → pituitary releases oxytocin → stronger contractions → more pressure → even more oxytocin … until the baby is born.
  - **Blood clotting** (Campbell p. 9): platelets gather at a wound and release chemicals that attract more platelets, until the clot seals the wound.
  - **Ice–albedo** (teacher's slide 7): warming melts ice → darker surface absorbs more energy → more warming → more melting.
  - **Permafrost** (slide 8) and **soil decomposition** (slide 10): warming releases more greenhouse gases → more warming.
- **Memory hook:** positive = *adds to* the change. "Positive" doesn't mean "good." It means "in the same direction."

<!-- bookfig: c-40-9 -->


<!-- figure: 1B-childbirth -->


### experimental control
- **Definition:** the part of an experiment that gives you something to compare against. It usually means the **control group**, which "is exposed to the same conditions as the experimental group except for changes in the independent variable" (M&L p. 12). It also refers to keeping all other variables **controlled** (held constant).
- **Why it matters:** without a control you can't tell whether your independent variable caused the result or something else did. In the salt marsh experiment, the no-nitrogen plots were the control (M&L p. 11).
- **Example tied to 1B:** to test whether a sports drink helps keep body temperature stable during exercise, one group drinks the sports drink (experimental). The control group drinks plain water, with the same exercise, room temperature, and time. Only the drink differs.
- **Don't confuse:** a *control group* (the comparison group) with *controlled variables* (factors kept the same in both groups). Both are part of experimental control.

## Part 4. Academic vocabulary

### condition
- **Meaning:** the state something is in, or a factor in the surroundings. *Internal conditions* are inside the body (temperature, water content). *External conditions* are outside (air temperature, sunlight).
- **In 1B:** "most organisms must keep internal conditions, such as temperature and water content, fairly constant to survive" (M&L p. 24). In a feedback diagram, the **condition** is the box in the middle that's being regulated (teacher's slide 4).
- **In experiments:** "a control group is exposed to the same *conditions* as the experimental group" (M&L p. 12).

### constant
- **Meaning (adjective):** staying the same, not changing. **(noun):** something that stays the same, such as "the constants in an experiment."
- **In 1B:** homeostasis keeps conditions "fairly constant" (M&L p. 24) or "relatively constant" (M&L p. 907). The words *fairly* and *relatively* matter, because conditions move within a range rather than being perfectly fixed.
- **In experiments:** controlled variables are held *constant*.

### criteria
- **Meaning:** standards used to judge or decide something. *Criteria* is plural; the singular is *criterion*.
- **In 1B:** "What are the **criteria** to determine if something is alive?" The criteria are the eight characteristics of life. A thing must meet *all* of them to be classified as living.
- **Example sentence:** "Fire meets some criteria for life, such as using energy and growing, but it does not meet the criteria of being made of cells or having a genetic code."

### dynamic
- **Meaning:** always changing or active. The opposite of *static*.
- **In 1B:** homeostasis is a **dynamic equilibrium**, meaning balance kept by constant adjustment (teacher's slide 2; Campbell p. 894). The textbook also calls the process of science "dynamic" because its steps loop back on themselves (M&L p. 15).
- **Picture it:** a driver keeps "slightly moving the wheel left and right" to stay in the lane (M&L p. 907). The car stays centered, but only because of constant small corrections. That's dynamic stability.

### equilibrium
- **Meaning:** a state of balance, where opposing forces or processes cancel each other out.
- **In 1B:** the root *stasis* in homeostasis means "a state of balance or equilibrium" (M&L p. 907). In **dynamic equilibrium**, things keep moving (you eat and excrete every day), but the overall level stays balanced (your weight stays within a few pounds; teacher's slide 2).
- **Static vs. dynamic equilibrium:** a book resting on a table is static equilibrium (nothing moving). Your body temperature is dynamic equilibrium (constant heating and cooling that balance out).

<!-- figure: 1B-static-dynamic -->


### mechanism
- **Meaning:** the process or set of parts by which something happens; *how* it works.
- **In 1B:** "The way organisms maintain homeostasis is through **feedback mechanisms**" (teacher's slide 2). Shivering and sweating are the mechanisms for body temperature. M&L's crosscutting concept "Cause and Effect: **Mechanism** and Explanation" (p. 25) means that science explains events by finding the mechanism that causes them.
- **Example sentence:** "Sweating is a cooling mechanism: evaporation of sweat removes heat from the skin."

### model
- **Meaning:** a simplified representation of an object, system, or idea. It can be physical, a drawing, a diagram, a flowchart, a math equation, or a computer simulation (M&L p. 21).
- **In 1B:**
  - The **feedback loop diagram** you draw in the Feedback practice is a model.
  - The **thermostat** is used as a model for homeostasis, "a nonliving system that keeps conditions within a certain range" (M&L p. 907).
- **Why scientists use models:** "Models help visualize and summarize ideas and communicate ideas to others" (M&L p. 21). All models leave something out. The thermostat model, for example, has one sensor, but the body uses many.

<!-- bookfig: c-models -->


### range
- **Meaning:** the span between a lower limit and an upper limit.
- **In 1B:** homeostasis keeps conditions "within a certain range, never allowing them to go too far one way or the other" (M&L p. 907). Normal human body temperature is about 36–38 °C; normal blood glucose is 70–110 mg/100 mL (Campbell p. 893).
- **Why it matters:** the teacher's slide 2 says the stable state "is usually not entirely rigid, but can fluctuate within a certain range." Going *outside* the range is when problems start, such as fever, hypothermia, or low blood sugar.

### reinforce
- **Meaning:** to strengthen or add support to something.
- **In 1B:** positive feedback is when "an end product **reinforces** or strengthens the stimulus" (Campbell p. 9). The Feedback practice asks for "a **self-reinforcing** loop," a loop that keeps strengthening itself.
- **Example sentence:** "Melting ice exposes dark water, which absorbs more heat and reinforces the warming."
- **Everyday use:** practice reinforces learning (a 1A idea).

### Q1. What are the criteria to determine if something is alive?

**Short answer:** No single trait is enough. A living thing shows *all* of these characteristics: it is made of cells, reproduces, has a universal genetic code (DNA), grows and develops, obtains and uses materials and energy, responds to its environment, maintains a stable internal environment (homeostasis), and evolves over time as a group (M&L p. 22).

**Each criterion explained**

| # | Criterion | What it means | Example |
|--|----------|--------------------|------------|
| 1 | **Made of cells** | Cells are "the smallest units of an organism that can be considered alive" (M&L p. 23). Organisms are unicellular (one cell) or multicellular (many cells). | Bacteria (1 cell); humans (trillions) |
| 2 | **Reproduce** | Organisms make new organisms. **Sexual reproduction:** two parents. **Asexual reproduction:** one parent (M&L p. 23). | Budding yeast (asexual); dogs (sexual) |
| 3 | **Universal genetic code** | Inherited instructions are carried in DNA, and the same code works in nearly every organism on Earth (M&L p. 23). | Offspring resemble their parents |
| 4 | **Grow and develop** | Organisms get bigger, and multicellular organisms *develop*: cells divide and differentiate into different types (M&L p. 23). | Egg → caterpillar → monarch butterfly |
| 5 | **Obtain and use materials and energy** | They take in matter and energy and use them in chemical reactions: **metabolism** (M&L p. 24). | Plants use sunlight; animals eat food |
| 6 | **Respond to the environment** | They detect and react to **stimuli**, both external (light, temperature) and internal (thirst) (M&L p. 24). | Sunflowers turning to follow the sun |
| 7 | **Maintain a stable internal environment** | **Homeostasis**: internal conditions stay within a safe range (M&L p. 24). | Sweating when hot |
| 8 | **Evolve** | A *group* of organisms changes over many generations. Individuals don't evolve (M&L p. 24). | Bacteria evolving antibiotic resistance |

Campbell's version (p. 3) lists seven properties that overlap closely: order (organized structure), reproduction, growth and development, energy processing, response to the environment, regulation (homeostasis), and evolutionary adaptation.

<!-- bookfig: c-metamorphosis -->


**Why you need all of them (testing nonliving things):**
- **Fire** grows, uses energy (burns fuel), gives off light, and "moves in its own way" (M&L p. 22). But it has no cells, no DNA, and no homeostasis, so it is **not alive**.
- **A car** moves, uses energy, and responds (to the driver), but has no cells, doesn't reproduce, and doesn't evolve. **Not alive.**
- **A crystal** grows, but has no cells or metabolism. **Not alive.**
- **A mule** is alive even though it's sterile (can't reproduce). It meets every other criterion and is made of cells from parents that did reproduce. The criteria describe living things *in general*, not every individual. (The What Is Life? notes ask: "Are there any living organisms that cannot reproduce?")

<!-- figure: 1B-life-checklist -->


**Viruses: the borderline case** (M&L p. 24; What Is Life? notes)

| Viruses share with living things | Viruses lack |
|---|---|
| have genetic material (DNA or RNA) | not made of cells, just proteins, nucleic acids, and sometimes lipids |
| can reproduce, but *only* inside a host cell | have no metabolism of their own |
| evolve (e.g., new flu strains each year) | can't grow or maintain homeostasis |
| can regulate gene expression | can't reproduce on their own |

Most biologists classify viruses as **nonliving** (or "at the border"), because they can't do anything outside a host cell. This question has no single agreed answer, and scientists weigh the evidence and argue both sides. That is exactly the kind of "explanation revised through argumentation" the 1B target describes. For a test, state your position and support it with specific criteria, CER style.

### Q2. Describe and give examples of metabolism.

**Definition:** Metabolism is **all of the chemical reactions that build up and break down molecules in living things** (teacher's note). The class textbook calls it "the combination of chemical reactions [an organism uses] as it carries out its life processes" (M&L p. 24). Campbell calls it "the totality of an organism's chemical reactions" (p. 146).

**Why organisms need it.** Just as "a building grows taller because workers use energy to assemble new materials" (M&L p. 24), organisms need *energy* and *materials* to grow, repair themselves, reproduce, and just stay alive. Metabolism is how they get energy out of food and use it to build what they need.

**Two kinds of metabolic reactions** (Campbell p. 146):
1. **Breaking down (catabolic):** big, complex molecules are broken into smaller ones, which **releases energy**.
2. **Building up (anabolic):** small molecules are assembled into bigger ones, which **uses energy**.

Energy released by breakdown reactions powers the building reactions.

**Examples**

| Example | Build up or break down? | What happens | Where |
|----------|--------|--------------------|----------|
| **Photosynthesis** | Build up | Carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen. Light energy is stored as chemical energy in sugar. | Plants, algae (chloroplasts) |
| **Cellular respiration** | Break down | Glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + **energy (ATP)**. This is the main way cells release usable energy. | Nearly all organisms (mitochondria) |
| **Protein synthesis** | Build up | Amino acids are joined into proteins, following instructions in DNA. | All cells (ribosomes) |
| **Digestion** | Break down | Large food molecules (starch, proteins, fats) → small molecules (sugars, amino acids, fatty acids) the body can absorb. | Digestive system |
| **Storing glucose as glycogen** | Build up | After a meal, insulin tells liver and muscle cells to link glucose into glycogen (M&L p. 932). | Liver, muscles |
| **Breaking down glycogen** | Break down | Between meals, glucagon tells the liver to release glucose (M&L p. 932). | Liver |


<!-- bookfig: c-energyflow -->

**Connections:**
- **To homeostasis:** metabolism produces heat. When you're cold, the hypothalamus signals cells to "speed up their activities," and the heat from "cellular respiration" raises body temperature (M&L p. 908). When you're hot, the body slows metabolism down.
- **To ecology (1C):** photosynthesis and cellular respiration are opposites, and together they move energy and carbon through ecosystems. Producers build sugar by photosynthesis; consumers break it down by respiration.

### Q3. How do feedback mechanisms help to maintain homeostasis?

**Short answer:** Feedback mechanisms constantly detect changes in internal conditions and trigger responses that push the condition back toward normal. Mostly this happens through **negative feedback**, which opposes a change. Because every drift away from the set point is corrected, conditions like temperature, water, and blood sugar stay within a safe **range**, which is homeostasis.

**1. What homeostasis needs.** Homeostasis means keeping internal conditions "relatively constant … despite changes in internal and external environments" (M&L p. 907). The outside world keeps changing (hot days, meals, exercise), so the body needs a *control system* that keeps checking and correcting. That system is a **feedback loop**: "a condition triggers some action that causes a change in that same condition" (teacher's slide 2).

**2. How a negative feedback loop works.** Every loop has the same parts (Campbell pp. 893–894):
- a **sensor** detects the condition
- a **control center** compares it to the **set point**
- an **effector** produces a **response** that pushes the condition the opposite way

Typically there are two loops working together: "mechanisms that lower a condition when it is too high, and other mechanisms that raise the level when it is too low" (teacher's slide 2).

**3. Example: body temperature** (teacher's slide 4; M&L p. 908)

<!-- figure: temp-loop -->

In words:
- **Too hot** (exercise, hot day): nerve cells in the hypothalamus detect it → sweat glands produce sweat, and cellular activity slows → evaporation cools the skin → temperature **falls** back toward 37 °C.
- **Too cold:** the hypothalamus detects it → cells speed up metabolism, and muscles shiver → heat is released → temperature **rises** back toward 37 °C.
- Each response **opposes** the change that triggered it, which is why it's *negative* feedback. When the temperature is back in range, the response switches off, just like a thermostat turning off a furnace (M&L p. 907).

<!-- bookfig: c-thermo -->


**4. Example: blood glucose** (M&L p. 932; Campbell p. 9)
- **After a meal**, blood glucose rises → the pancreas releases **insulin** → liver and muscle cells take up glucose and store it as glycogen → blood glucose **falls**.
- **Between meals**, blood glucose falls → the pancreas releases **glucagon** → the liver breaks down glycogen and releases glucose → blood glucose **rises**.
- Result: blood glucose stays around 70–110 mg per 100 mL (Campbell p. 893).

<!-- bookfig: c-glucose -->


**5. Example: water balance** (M&L pp. 24, 932)
- **Sweating** makes you lose water, so dissolved materials in the blood become more concentrated → the hypothalamus detects this → it triggers ADH (kidneys save water) and **thirst** (you drink) → water level **rises** back to normal.
- **After drinking** a lot, less ADH is released → the kidneys remove more water → the level **falls** back.

**6. Why it's a *dynamic* equilibrium.** Responses aren't instant, so conditions wobble a little above and below the set point before being corrected. Homeostasis "moderates but doesn't eliminate changes" (Campbell p. 894). Stability comes from *constant correction*, like a driver steering to stay in the lane (M&L p. 907).

**7. What about positive feedback?** Positive feedback *amplifies* a change instead of reversing it, so it does **not** maintain homeostasis. In the body it's used only for processes that need to *finish*: childbirth contractions and blood clotting (Campbell pp. 9, 894). In natural systems it's often harmful, because it pushes things to extremes (teacher's slide 3). The climate examples from class:

| Slide | Loop | Type | Why |
|---|------------------|-----|--------------|
| 6 | More CO₂ → warmer → more photosynthesis → CO₂ removed → cooling | **Negative** | The result opposes the warming. |
| 7 | Warmer → ice melts → lower albedo → more heat absorbed → warmer | **Positive** | The result adds to the warming. |
| 8 | Warmer → permafrost melts → methane released → warmer | **Positive** | The result adds to the warming. |
| 9 | Warmer → more evaporation → more clouds → more reflection → cooler | **Negative** | The result opposes the warming. |
| 10 | Warmer → faster decomposition → more CO₂ → warmer | **Positive** | The result adds to the warming. |

**How to tell them apart:** follow the loop all the way around. If you end up pushing the original condition *back*, it's negative. If you push it *further the same way*, it's positive.

**8. When feedback fails.** "Any breakdown of homeostasis may have serious or even fatal consequences" (M&L p. 25). If sweating can't keep up on a very hot day, body temperature climbs out of range (heatstroke). If the insulin loop doesn't work, blood glucose stays high (diabetes).

**Tips for the Feedback practice worksheet**
- For **negative feedback**, put a *measurable* condition in the center box and draw two loops, one for "too high" and one for "too low." Everyday examples: water temperature in a shower (too hot → turn the cold up; too cold → turn the hot up), or hunger level (hungry → eat → full → stop eating).
- For **positive feedback**, make sure every arrow makes the condition *more extreme*. Everyday examples: a microphone near a speaker (sound → amplified → picked up again → louder squeal), or a rumor spreading (more people know → more people tell others → even more people know).
- Don't use technical examples you found online, such as blood glucose. The worksheet asks for your own everyday examples.
